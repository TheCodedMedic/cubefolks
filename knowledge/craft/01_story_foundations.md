# 01. Story foundations (for any video that tells a story)

The hooks guide in `../STORYTELLING_HOOKS_GUIDE.md` is built from ten videos by one creator and is strong on **hooks and attention**. It is thin on what makes a story *work underneath the hooks*. This file fills that gap with well-established story craft. Sources are named so you can read the originals; everything here is paraphrased in our own words.

Read order for a new Short: `../SHORTS_PLAYBOOK.md`, this file, then `02_attention_and_psychology.md`.

---

## 1. A story is a change that costs something

The smallest useful definition: **a character wants something, something stands in the way, they try, they fail in a way that changes the situation, and in the end they are different (or the world is).** Without a want there is no direction; without an obstacle there is no tension; without change there is no point.

Test any idea with one sentence (the Pixar "story spine" and McKee's "controlling idea" both end up here):

> *Once there was ___. Every day ___. One day ___. Because of that ___. Because of that ___. Until finally ___. And ever since ___.*

If you cannot fill it in, you have a topic, not a story. (Works for fictions, histories and explainers: an explainer's "character" is the viewer or the thing being explained.)

Our example: *The microwave.* Once there was an engineer who built radar parts. Every day he stood next to a humming radar tube. One day the chocolate bar in his pocket melted. Because of that he tried popcorn, then an egg. Because of that he saw the tube could cook from the inside. Until finally his company turned a war machine into a kitchen machine. And ever since, almost every kitchen has one.

## 2. The five things every story beat needs

For every beat (a paragraph of narration, a scene), you should be able to answer:

1. **Who** is the viewer following right now?
2. **What do they want** in this beat?
3. **What stops them?**
4. **What changes** by the end of the beat (information, power, mood, relationship, position)? If nothing changes, cut or merge the beat.
5. **What question does it leave open** so the next beat is wanted? (The re-hook. Close one gap and open the next in the same breath.)

"But / therefore" (Parker & Stone's rule) is the quick test: between two beats you must be able to say *but* or *therefore*. If you can only say *and then*, the beats are a list, not a story.

## 3. Structures you can use (pick one deliberately)

| Structure | Shape | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Three acts** (setup, confrontation, resolution) | ~25% / 50% / 25%, with a turning point at each break and a midpoint reversal | most narrative videos 3-15 min |
| **Five-beat spine** (hook, rising trouble, crisis, climax, aftermath) | compact version of the above | short videos and Shorts |
| **Dan Harmon's story circle** (you, need, go, search, find, take, return, change) | circular, character-change centred | adventures, "a day in the life" with a change |
| **Hero's journey** (Campbell, Vogler) | departure, initiation, return | epics, origin stories, "how X came to be" |
| **Save the Cat beats** (Blake Snyder) | 15 beats with timings (opening image, catalyst, break into two, midpoint, all is lost, finale, final image) | feature-like scripts; useful as a timing checklist |
| **Kishōtenketsu** (ki, shō, ten, ketsu) | introduce, develop, **twist with no conflict**, reconcile | gentle, surprising, no-villain stories; good for short explainers |
| **Vonnegut shapes** | plot the hero's fortune over time: man in a hole, boy meets girl, Cinderella, "from bad to worse" | quick check that your arc moves up and down, not flat |
| **Try-fail cycles** | attempt, fail with a new complication, attempt again, bigger | thrillers, heists, puzzles, escalating "and then it got worse" stories |
| **Problem, investigation, answer, implication** | question-driven | explainers and documentaries |
| **Rank / ladder** | steps up or down, each with a surprise | "every rank", "levels of" videos |

Rules of thumb: put the **first turning point inside the first 20-25% of the runtime**; make the **midpoint a reversal** (the plan was wrong, the truth is bigger); give the **lowest point just before the end** ("all is lost") so the climax is earned; give the **ending a new normal** (what is different now).

## 4. Scenes: goal, conflict, outcome

Build each scene (a place and time) as: **goal** of the viewpoint character, **conflict** (someone or something resists), **outcome** that is *no / yes-but / no-and / yes-and*. A scene that ends in a plain "yes" kills momentum. Prefer **"no, and worse"** early and **"yes, but at a cost"** late. (Swain's scene-and-sequel; Ingermanson.)

Enter late, leave early: start the scene at the last interesting moment, end as soon as the outcome is clear (a cut on the reaction beats a cut after it).

## 5. Setup and payoff (the machine that makes endings land)

- **Plant** a fact early in plain sight, **pay it off** later with a twist of meaning (Chekhov's gun). Plant at least **twice** if the payoff is big, once as a joke or a detail, once as a rule.
- A twist is satisfying when it is **surprising but inevitable in hindsight**: two clues planted, one false explanation offered (the head fake), then the true one.
- **Callbacks**: the last image or line answers the first. Ours: every Short's last line runs straight back into its first line, and the last shot cuts into the first.
- Name the idea (the Clever Hans effect, a perverse incentive) the first time it appears, so a graphic and a later callback can use the same words.

## 6. Character in one page

A short video has no time for biography. Give each important character, in order:
1. **A want you can see** (the girl wants her plane back; the boss wants an answer).
2. **A flaw or limit** that causes trouble (he never finished school; he grabbed the wrong part).
3. **A visual identity readable in silhouette** (scarf, buns, fedora, glasses) and **a voice** (rhythm, vocabulary, a catch phrase).
4. **A change or a reveal** (even small).

The **villain** can be a thing or a system (time, a rule, a rival idea). "Hero vs nothing" is weak; give the want a resistance.

For the narrator: pick a **persona** (calm friend, deadpan expert, conspiratorial storyteller), a stance on the viewer (second-person "you" puts them inside), and keep it consistent. Humour comes from the narrator's attitude towards what is happening, not from labelled jokes.

## 7. Theme without lecturing

Decide what the story is *about* in one line (not the plot; the claim): *You do not need more time, you need to know where to look.* Then let events prove it. State it **once**, plainly, near the end (a short line), and never before the audience has felt it.

## 8. Stakes and emotion

- Stakes = **a character to root for + something at risk + a clock**. They do not have to be life and death; they have to be **personally relevant to the viewer**.
- Make the audience **care before you hurt**: spend a beat of warmth or humour with the person at risk first (the proud horse snorts before the scientists arrive).
- **Contrast** drives emotion: a joyful scene then a sudden quiet; a calm voice at the worst moment, or a short silence before the reveal.
- Let the aftermath **breathe**: the beat after the climax (the hug, the shaking) is what the viewer remembers.

## 9. Endings

Plan the ending first. A good ending (a) **closes the main question**, (b) shows the **cost or the change**, (c) has **one memorable last line**, (d) optionally **opens the next loop** (a series hook), and (e) **echoes the opening**. Do not end on a summary or on "thanks for watching": end on the image and the line, then the call to action as a short coda.

## 10. Humour in story

- **Rule of three**: two similar, the third breaks the pattern.
- **Misdirection**: lead the expectation one way, pay off the other (the sandwich was taken by a pigeon, not a thief).
- **Understatement and deadpan**: the narrator reports the disaster flatly (`Tap, tap. Nothing.`).
- **Running gag with escalation**, then a **callback** at the end.
- **Specificity** is funnier than generality (`six fifty-eight a.m.` beats `morning`).
- **Timing**: a joke needs a beat of silence or a cut on the reaction; never talk over the punchline.
- Release tension with a gag **after** every big peak so the audience can breathe.

## 11. Common structural failures and fixes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starts slow | warm-up, background first | start at the first interesting moment; background arrives only when needed |
| Middle sags | list of events, no turning point, no escalation | add a reversal; make each attempt cost more; add a clock |
| Ending feels flat | main question not paid, or paid at once | delay one beat, add the cost, add the echo |
| Twist feels cheap | no clues | plant two clues earlier, one false explanation |
| No one cares | no want, no stakes | give a specific want and something to lose |
| Confusing | too many new ideas | one new concept per beat; name it; repeat it twice in different words |
| Preachy | theme stated early and often | show it; state it once at the end |
