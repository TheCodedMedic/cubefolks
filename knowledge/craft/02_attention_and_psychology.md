# 02. Attention and psychology (why people keep watching)

The hooks guide gives the creator-level rules. This file explains the mechanisms underneath, says how solid each one is, and turns each into something you can do in a script, a shot or a sound. **Use these to serve the story, never to trick the viewer**: a promise you do not keep loses the audience and the platform's trust (high click-through with low retention is punished).

## 1. The mechanisms (and how much to trust them)

| Mechanism | What it says | Evidence | Use it like this |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Information gap** (Loewenstein, 1994) | Curiosity is the feeling of a gap between what we know and what we want to know; it grows when we know *a little*. | well supported | Give enough to make the question clear, withhold the answer: "The pigeons scattered two seconds before it arrived." (What does that mean?) |
| **Open loops / unfinished tasks** (Zeigarnik effect) | Unfinished things stay in mind. | mixed in replications; still a good writing model | Open the next question before you close the current one. |
| **Prediction and surprise** | Attention spikes when outcomes break predictions; constant predictability bores. | well supported (prediction-error research) | Vary the pattern every 20-60 s: new place, new rule, reversal, a gag. |
| **Narrative transportation** (Green & Brock) | Viewers absorbed in a story are less likely to leave and more likely to be moved. | well supported | Concrete details, a character's point of view, continuous causality. |
| **Identification / second person** | Viewers attend to what is about *them*. | well supported for self-reference effects | "You" narration, everyday stakes (a missed train, a sandwich). |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses feel bigger than equal gains. | supported, size debated | Frame stakes as what could be lost ("the city is paying for MORE rats"). |
| **Peak-end rule** (Kahneman) | Experiences are remembered by the peak and the end. | well supported | Spend effort on the climax and the final 20 seconds. |
| **Cognitive load** (Sweller, Mayer) | Too much at once overloads working memory. | well supported | One new idea per beat; picture and words say the same thing; cut decorative text. |
| **Signalling** (Mayer) | Cues that point to what matters improve understanding. | well supported | Rings, push-ins, a highlight on the key object, a chip with the one number. |
| **Redundancy / modality** (Mayer) | Narration + matching visual beats narration + the same text on screen. | well supported | Don't caption what the narrator is saying; show a picture or a number instead. |
| **Social proof, authority** | People trust what others trust. | supported | A "real rule" aside, a measured number, a credible source line. |
| **Novelty / the "illusion of novelty"** | The familiar told from a new angle feels new. | craft heuristic | Pick a rare lens: a familiar topic through an unusual character or a single day. |

## 2. The attention curve of a video (what to do when)

| Time | What the viewer is deciding | Your tools |
|---|---|---|
| 0-3 s | "Is this for me?" | the key visual, the topic in sentence one, contrast, sound from frame one |
| 3-30 s | "Will this pay off?" | lock-in: confirm the promise, show proof, introduce the character and stakes |
| 30 s-2 min | "Is this going somewhere?" | the big question, first turning point, first re-hook |
| middle | "Is it still worth it?" | escalation, re-hooks every 60-90 s (20-30 s in shorts), pattern breaks, gags after peaks |
| last 20% | "Was it worth it?" | climax, payoff, cost, memorable line, echo of the opening |

**Retention diagnosis** (when you have analytics): a cliff in the first 30 s = the hook or lock-in failed; a slow slide = no re-hooks; a dip at one moment = confusion or a dull scene there (re-cut that moment); a bump = something people rewatch (do more of that).

## 3. Pattern breaks (and why they work)

- **Cut rhythm**: in our Shorts a new picture every 1-1.5 s, steady; the eye always has something new.
- **Scale changes**: wide, medium, close, macro insert, back to wide.
- **Silence**: a designed silence after the biggest moment is the strongest pattern break of all.
- **Speed changes**: slow-time beats (flash, rings, bubbles) against fast montage.
- **Visual devices**: a counter icon that changes state, a chip that pops, bars that tighten.
- **Voice changes**: a character's spoken line interrupting narration.

## 4. Packaging (title and thumbnail) is part of the story

The promise starts before the video. Write the title and thumbnail idea **with the hook**, so the first frame confirms what the thumbnail promised. One clear subject, one question or contrast, readable at phone size. (The guide's sections 4.5-4.7 cover alignment of the hook layers.) Never promise what the video does not contain.

## 5. Ethics and platform rules

- No fake urgency, no lies in the title, no misleading thumbnails.
- Disclose synthetic or altered content when a viewer could think it is real (YouTube's disclosure setting).
- True stories and explainers keep their facts accurate (cite sources in the description). Made-up stories are fine and need no
  fact-check, but are never presented as documented history, and never invent crimes or scandals for real, named people.
- Kids' content: follow the platform's made-for-kids rules.
