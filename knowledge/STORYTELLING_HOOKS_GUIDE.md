## 1. The one idea underneath everything

**Attention is a gap that the viewer wants closed.**

Every source, in different words, says the same thing:

- A **curiosity gap** opens when the viewer expects one thing and is shown something else. The creator calls the distance between what they expect (A) and what you show (B) **contrast** [E][F][J].
- The bigger and clearer the gap, the more they lean in. The gap must be *understandable* (they must grasp A and B), or it becomes confusion instead of curiosity [B][C][D].
- The gap is closed by an **answer**. A story that never closes its gaps feels unfinished; a story that closes every gap the moment it opens feels flat. The craft is to **close one gap while opening the next in the same breath** [C][H][I].
- Between opening and closing, the viewer **guesses**. The guessing is the pleasure. So you must give them enough clear facts to guess with [B][C].
- When the answer breaks their guess in a way that makes sense afterwards, they get the biggest reward. That is a good surprise. A surprise that makes no sense is just noise [C].

A flat story is one in which the viewer's brain predicts correctly all the time. The brain gets bored of being right [J]. A good storyteller keeps the viewer slightly off balance, in a way that stays fair and clear.

> **The whole craft in one sentence:** *Make the viewer care, give them a clear question, let them guess, then answer in a way that is surprising but obvious in hindsight, and open the next question before they can leave.*

## 2. The master rules

These are the merged rules, numbered for reference. Detail and examples follow in later sections.

### Hooks (first 1-5 seconds, first 1-2 sentences)

1. **A hook has one job:** make the viewer decide to keep watching. Nothing else [D].
2. **The hook must deliver two things:** topic clarity (what is this about?) and on-target curiosity (is it for me, and what comes next?) [D]. In interrupt-theory terms: *subject* and *outcome* [G].
3. **Say what the video is about in the first sentence.** Do not delay the topic. Cut every opening line that is warm-up [D][A].
4. **Never open with a vague teaser** like "you won't believe this" or "this is crazy". It says nothing, so the viewer cannot decide [D][A].
5. **Be crystal clear.** Short words, active voice, one possible meaning. If the viewer must think for half a second, they swipe [D][G].
6. **Use "you/your" in the hook, not "I/me/my"**, so the viewer feels the story is for them [D].
7. **Lead with the pain or the benefit** the viewer already cares about, not a neutral fact about the topic [D][E].
8. **Create contrast in the hook:** what they believe (A) versus what you show (B) [D][E][F].
9. **Use the 3-step formula:** context lean, then a scroll-stop interjection (usually "but"), then a contrarian snapback [E].
10. **Make the hook sentences short and staccato.** Expand to medium and long sentences later [E].
11. **Show while you tell.** The first thing seen should confirm the first thing said [A][E][F][G].
12. **Align all hook layers:** spoken line, visual, on-screen text, and sound must say the same thing [F][G].
13. **The key visual comes first.** Pick the strongest image for the first seconds before you write the line [F].
14. **If you have no strong visual and no clear contrast, do not make the video yet.** Pick a better idea [F].
15. **Put the first hit of value inside or right after the hook.** Do not save your best material for later [E][D].

### The seconds after the hook

16. **The lock-in zone** (the next two to four sentences) matters as much as the hook. Use it to (a) confirm the hook's promise and (b) earn trust quickly [G].
17. **Confirm the promise** so nobody feels baited and switched [G].
18. **Prove it fast.** A concrete detail, example, or proof builds trust [G].

### Structure

19. **Start the story already at high intensity.** Not at zero. The modern story begins around 70 out of 100 [I].
20. **Introduce the first conflict or contrast early** (within the first minute or two of a longer video) [I].
21. **Release tension, then build again.** Peaks and valleys, with a new peak roughly every 2-5 minutes in long-form [I].
22. **Open with *what* and *why it matters*, then *who* and *how we know*, then *where and when*.** The where/when is icing, not the cake [I].
23. **Connect beats with "but" and "therefore", never "and then".** "And then" is a list; "but/therefore" is causation and conflict [A].
24. **Run the addiction loop on repeat:** stakes, big question, head fake, re-hook [C].
25. **Re-hook before every section ends.** Close one loop and open the next in the same sentence [C][H][I].
26. **Re-hook cadence:** about every 20-30 seconds in short videos; about every 60-90 seconds in long ones; a misdirection about every 2-3 minutes [H][J].
27. **Plan the ending first,** then write backwards [A].
28. **Close the main loop.** Do not end on an unexplained cliffhanger unless it is a deliberate series hook [B].

### Characters and stakes

29. **Stakes need three things:** a character to root for, something at risk, and urgency [C].
30. **Stakes do not have to be life or death.** They just have to be *personally relevant* to the viewer [C].
31. **Give the story a villain** (it can be a thing, a system, a rival idea). Hero versus nothing is weak; hero versus villain is contrast [I].
32. **Give the viewer something to root for;** let them see themselves in the hero [I][J].

### Language

33. **Name your ideas** (term branding). A named thing feels important and creates curiosity [H].
34. **Speak as if it is already true.** Replace "if, maybe, might, could" with "when, the reason, once you" [H].
35. **Say what the viewer is thinking** (thought narration) at the moment they think it [H].
36. **Flip advice into warnings** (negative frames). "Do not do this" pulls harder than "do this" [H].
37. **Use contrast words** (but, actually, instead, turns out, except, yet) to turn statements into gaps [H].
38. **Vary sentence length** to create rhythm. A jagged right edge in your script is good [A].
39. **Write like you talk to one friend.** Conversational tone beats polished tone [A].
40. **Make the core idea small enough to retell** in one sentence (atomic sharability) [I].

## 4. HOOKS

### 4.1 What a hook is for

A hook is the first one to three sentences (and the first one to five seconds of picture) of a video. Its job is not to explain or to impress. Its job is to **get the viewer to opt in** [D].

Two ideas appear in every source:

- **Viewers decide fast and leave quickly.** Short-form retention falls steeply in the first two seconds, like a cliff, and in longer YouTube videos a large share of viewers are gone in the first 30 seconds [D][I]. Everything you make after they leave "might as well be a black screen" [E].
- **A hook must be read by the brain in an instant.** The viewer sees first, then hears, then looks back at the picture to confirm what they heard. The creator calls this the visual-audio-visual sandwich [F].

The hook must give exactly two things [D]:

1. **Topic clarity:** the viewer knows what the video is about.
2. **On-target curiosity:** the viewer believes it is for them and wants to know what comes next.

Interrupt theory says the same thing as two silent questions the hook must answer in the viewer's head: *What is this about?* and *What will I get if I keep watching?* (subject and outcome) [G].

### 4.2 The four hook mistakes

If a hook fails, it is one of these four, or several [D]. Diagnose in this order.

**Mistake 1: Delay.** The topic arrives too late.
- Symptom: the first line or two are warm-up ("Guys, this is one of the craziest things I've ever seen").
- Why it fails: a "crazy thing" could be anything. The viewer has nothing to decide on.
- Fix: cut the warm-up, move the topic line to the top, state the topic inside the first 1-2 seconds. The creator calls this **speed to value** [D].
- Exception: vague openers can work when the person's face, emotion, or an on-screen text hook is doing the real work. The spoken words add nothing in those cases [D].
- Our example: instead of "You will not believe what this man found in his pocket", say "In 1945, a chocolate bar melted in a man's pocket, and it gave the world the microwave."

**Mistake 2: Confusion.** The viewer cannot parse what you said.
- Symptom: tangled sentences, passive voice, jargon, a long setup before the point.
- Fix: fewer words but enough that nobody can misunderstand; simple words (aim for about a sixth-grade reading level); active voice ("the dog jumped", not "the jump of the dog") [D].
- Test: read the hook alone, with nothing else. Could a viewer take it two ways? If yes, rewrite until only one reading remains [D].
- Related: keep the sentence short enough to hold in the head. An eight-word hook is easier to absorb than a 28-word one [G].

**Mistake 3: Irrelevance.** The viewer understands the topic but does not feel it is for them.
- Fix A: say **you/your** instead of **I/me/my**. "I struggled with skin problems my whole life" invites the question "do I have what you have?" while "If you've struggled with skin problems your whole life" tells them directly [D].
- Fix B: **frame around expected value.** Entertainment solves boredom; education solves a pain. Lead with the pain or the benefit rather than a neutral fact. "Three skincare trends" is a nice-to-have; "If you have acne, try these three things" is a need-to-have [D][E].
- The same idea appears as: *lead with the benefit and introduce the topic as the solution* (example from [E]: "if you want better sleep, you need magnesium", not "magnesium is one of the essential minerals").

**Mistake 4: Disinterest.** The viewer is clear and the topic is relevant, but not curious enough.
- Fix: build a **curiosity loop** with **contrast**.
- A curiosity loop is: they see something, a question forms, you give a little context that answers it but triggers a new question, and so on [D].
- Contrast is the distance between what they believe now (A) and the alternative you show (B) [D][F].
- Two types of contrast [D]:
  - **Stated:** say both A and B out loud. "Most people fix X with the slow method, but there is a version that works three times faster."
  - **Implied:** say only B, because everyone already knows A. "This is eight times more effective than the usual fix."
- In practice: the first sentence gives clarity, the next one or two give contrast. A hook is usually two to three lines, not one [D].

### 4.3 The 3-step hook formula [E]

The creator calls this his main formula. It is the most reusable single tool in the set.

**Step 1: Context lean (one to two lines).**
- Do two things: (a) be crystal clear about the topic so the right viewers stay and the wrong ones leave; (b) get them leaning in.
- Ways to make them lean in: establish common ground, mention a benefit or pain they already have, use a metaphor that simplifies something complex, or tell them something that blows their mind.

**Step 2: Scroll-stop interjection (a single short line).**
- A stun-gun line that makes them stop moving forward, "like running into a window".
- The easiest version is a contrast word: **but**, however, yet, although, therefore, on the other hand. Often phrased like "But here's the thing" or "But get this".
- It sets up step 3. It does not deliver content itself.

**Step 3: Contrarian snapback (the haymaker).**
- A sentence that goes the opposite direction from the lean in step 1, while staying on topic. The bigger the shock, the bigger the snap.
- The viewer thinks "I thought it was X", the snapback says "it's actually Y", and now they must know why.

**Generic template**
> [Topic + something they care about]. [But / However / Here's the thing]. [The unexpected truth that reverses their assumption].

**Our-style example (history story)**
> The Royal Navy fed its sailors beer instead of water. But the beer was not the problem. The problem was where they kept it.

**Our-style example (invention origin)**
> In 1956, an engineer grabbed the wrong part from a box. But it didn't break his machine. It made it beat like a heart.

The creator also says this works beyond short video: in long-form, in conversation, in a sales pitch. It is basic "psychology 101": context lean, scroll-stop interjection, contrarian snapback [E].

**Warning from the source:** the snapback has to be *compelling*, and the video must pay it off. Otherwise you have created clickbait and viewers will leave angry [E].

**Boosters on top of the formula [E]:**
1. **Visual hooks.** On-screen text plus compelling motion plus the spoken line beats speech alone (see 4.5 and 8).
2. **Lead with benefit or pain** when introducing a topic (see Mistake 3).
3. **Culture hopping.** Wrap an unfamiliar idea in something people already know: a brand, a celebrity, a movement. Familiar things create comfort; too much unfamiliar material makes people feel dumb and leave. (The raw transcript spells this "cult hopping"; the meaning is "culture hopping".) Example: explain a complex financial idea through how a famous artist's accountant would handle it.
4. **Compress speed to value.** Give a small hit of real value in the hook or right after. Short-form timer is about 4 seconds; for YouTube it is more like 1-2 minutes before attention drops off a cliff.
5. **Make hook sentences staccato.** Very short in the hook; vary length later.

### 4.4 The six hook archetypes [F]

All six create contrast. Any topic can be told in any of the six. Choose the one with the biggest contrast for the story you have.

| # | Archetype | What it does | Contrast it builds | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fortune Teller** | Positions the present against a future change | "This is how things are now" versus "this will change everything" | News, inventions, trends, predictions |
| 2 | **Experimenter** | Shows a demo or experiment, peer to peer | "I used to do it this way" versus "now look what happens" | Demos, challenges, tests, "I tried X for 30 days" |
| 3 | **Teacher** | Frames a lesson, teacher to student | "You are failing at this" versus "here is the method that wins" | How-to, breakdowns, lessons from others |
| 4 | **Magician** | A visual or verbal stun gun: "look at that" | Forces attention onto one striking thing. Often combined with the other five | Any story; strong first frame; signature sound or phrase |
| 5 | **Investigator** | Reveals a secret or finding nobody knows | "You don't know this" versus "now you do" | Mysteries, hidden facts, research |
| 6 | **Contrarian** | States a belief that goes against common wisdom | Direct: "what you believe is wrong" | Opinion, myth-busting, strong takes |

**Notes on the Magician [F].**
- It is the odd one out: it can sit in front of any of the other five as the attention grabber.
- A **visual pacifier** is a small activity that holds just enough subconscious attention while the main message plays (hands stacking cups, someone doing their makeup, split-screen gameplay). It keeps the viewer from leaving while you set up the point.
- A **signature opener** (the same short phrase or sound every time) becomes a recognisable pattern. The creator opens with a short "check this out" phrase on many videos.

**The six applied to one topic** (the creator's backpack example, rewritten as a story topic: *a new ship's biscuit recipe*):
- Fortune teller: "This biscuit will change how every sailor eats."
- Experimenter: "I ate only ship biscuits for a week. The third day was the problem."
- Teacher: "If you're planning to cross the sea, here is how to store your biscuits."
- Magician: a rapid match-cut of ten biscuits, one jumps out of the pile.
- Investigator: "Nobody mentions the one thing wrong with this biscuit."
- Contrarian: "Everyone calls this the worst food in history. It's actually the smartest."

### 4.5 The four hook layers and how to align them [F][G]

A hook has up to four parts that all arrive at once:

1. **Spoken hook** (what you say).
2. **Visual hook** (what the viewer sees).
3. **Text hook** (words on screen that are not the captions).
4. **Audio hook** (music and sound effects).

[G] adds a fourth for platforms: the **caption hook**, the first line of the written caption (for us, the YouTube title and the first line of the description).

**The most important rule: they must align.** [F] says the difference between 500 and 500,000 views is often alignment. If the viewer can mute the sound and still guess the words, and cover the picture and still guess the picture, the layers are aligned [G]. Misalignment causes **comprehension loss**, and comprehension loss makes viewers leave [F].

**How viewers actually take in a hook** [F]:
1. Eyes go to the image and any big text first.
2. The ears catch up and hear the spoken line.
3. Eyes go back to the picture to confirm what they heard.

Because eyes process far more than ears, the picture leads. Design the image so that the first glance already tells the story, and the words then confirm it.

### 4.6 The key-visual process [F]

The creator's five-step method for hooks. Use it before writing any line.

1. **Find the key visual.** Which image or moment in this story could fill the first 3-5 seconds? The better the visual, the easier the hook. If there is none, either manufacture one (motion graphics, a drawn scene) or drop the idea.
2. **Pick the angle with the biggest contrast,** then choose the hook archetype that expresses it.
3. **Write the spoken hook** (two to four lines) with the 3-step formula.
4. **Add the on-screen text** to support the image and to re-confirm after the words.
5. **Gut check comprehension:** go visual, then speech, then visual again. Is it perfectly clear what this video is about? If not, rework it. If it still does not work, throw the idea out; there are always more ideas.

**Case studies from the source (paraphrased):**
- *Good:* a video about full-size floor plans laid out in a warehouse. The image was something most people had never seen (instant contrast), the text said what it was with an arrow pointing at it, the spoken line described exactly what you saw ("you can walk through your home before you build it") and then offered a prediction (this is the future of home design). Everything pointed at one thing.
- *Bad:* a video about an abstract AI tool. The on-screen text said "future of storytelling", the picture showed unexplained rooms appearing, and the voice used technical terms nobody knew. The layers said three different things. The creator's own verdict: the idea should have been dropped or shown with clear, explicit visuals.

### 4.7 Writing the text hook [G]

On-screen title text is described as the single highest-leverage item, because eyes hunt for text and recognise key words faster than they can interpret a picture.

**Content of a text hook:** a **subject repeater** plus one of:
- a **pain-point reminder** ("people-pleasing"),
- a **desired-outcome teaser** ("ChatGPT will sell things for you"),
- a **future state change** ("Claude just changed social media forever").

**Rules**
1. Include a knowledge gap so curiosity is built in.
2. Be specific: "22 days" beats "a few weeks"; "$2,700" beats "a few thousand".
3. Use **power words**: short vivid phrases that compress a sentence (see 4.8).
4. At most **two lines** (three only with strong typographic hierarchy, where one line is big and the others are small).
5. Break lines where the phrase reads naturally. Never split a phrase such as "the future of work".
6. **No punctuation** except quotation marks and parentheses.
7. **No connecting words** such as and, that, but, therefore. A text hook is a trigger, not a sentence.
8. Leave it on screen for **about 3 seconds**, long enough to read, look at the picture, and read again.
9. Place it where the eyes go first (about a third down or near the middle), without covering faces or the key action.
10. Study the top 15-20 creators in the niche to understand the landscape, then remix, don't copy.

### 4.8 Writing the spoken hook: formats and power words [G]

**Level 1: hook formats.** A format is the grammatical shape of a sentence, with the specifics removed. Examples from the source: question hook, tutorial hook, problem-solution hook, "Here's how to do [big task] in [short time]". Process: find hooks that worked, remove the specifics to reveal the shape, then refill with your own subject.

**Level 2: power words.** Power words are one-to-three-word phrases that amplify an outcome or a pain ("heat-seeking missile", "social media machine", "scientifically impossible to skip"). They compress a sentence into a punch. Process: collect your top hooks, scan for repeated power phrases, then **remix** them: keep the psychology, change the words ("impossible to ignore", "impossible to resist"). Lazy creators copy the phrase; good ones remix it.

**Level 3: combine.** Remixed format plus remixed power words gives a new hook that is data-backed but original.

**Four ingredients of any hook (the "4 S's") [G]**
1. **Subject:** a crystal-clear person, thing, place, or mission.
2. **Stakes:** why the viewer should care (for entertainment: a curiosity-inducing scenario; for education: the outcome they want).
3. **Speed:** deliver subject and stakes in as few words as possible. "Compression, not velocity": do not talk faster, use fewer words.
4. **Super clear:** one possible interpretation. Simple words, no clever wordplay, no vague set-ups.

### 4.9 The lock-in zone [G]

The 5-10 seconds (two to four sentences) right after the hook. The creator argues it is *more* important than the hook: the hook gets a chance, the lock-in zone earns the stay. It does two jobs:

1. **Confirm the hook's claim,** so the viewer is not baited and switched.
2. **Rapid trust evaluation.** Viewers decide, in a few seconds, whether you will waste their time. Four levers:
   - **How you look / how it looks** (attractive, appealing presentation).
   - **Packaging quality:** visuals, framing, sound, polish. You don't need an expensive set, just visible care. Care signals trust.
   - **Relatability:** does the messenger feel like someone they would trust for this topic?
   - **Proof (the trust anchor):** a study, a case, a concrete detail, a screenshot, a source.

For our Shorts: the lock-in zone (about seconds 2-6) is where we show the character, the setting, a concrete fact (a name, a year, a number) and the first gag, all in the same breath, so the viewer sees the story is real.

### 4.10 Hook checklist

Run through this for every hook, in this order:

- [ ] Does the first sentence say what this is about? (no warm-up)
- [ ] Could a ten-year-old understand it? Only one meaning?
- [ ] Does it say *you*, or clearly aim at the viewer's interest or pain?
- [ ] Is there contrast (A versus B) in the first two or three lines?
- [ ] Is there a "but" moment that reverses the lean?
- [ ] Is the key visual strong, and does it show what the words say?
- [ ] Does the on-screen text add to it (no punctuation, two lines, subject plus gap)?
- [ ] Do visual, speech, text and sound all say the same thing?
- [ ] Is there a first hit of value inside or right after the hook?
- [ ] Does the lock-in zone confirm the promise and show proof or care?
- [ ] Would the video still make sense if the hook had to be rewritten as a title?

## 5. STORY STRUCTURE

### 5.1 The modern story arc [I]

The school arc (setup, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution) is one slow bell curve. The creator argues it fails for online video because viewers have "time compression": they will not wait for the rising action. 80% may bounce before it gets interesting.

**The modern arc** (the creator jokes about calling it by his own name):
1. **Start high.** The intro begins at about 70 out of 100 intensity, not 0, and carries context and characters at that level.
2. **First conflict or contrast early,** inside the first minute or two, spiking to about 90.
3. **Release.** Tension eases (to about 30) so the viewer can breathe.
4. **Build again** to another conflict or head fake (about 75).
5. **Repeat** the peak-and-release wave on a **2-5 minute cadence** until the end.
6. The biggest peak can be early (later peaks ramp down) or late (peaks ramp up).

Compared with the school arc: the intro starts above zero, the first conflict comes much earlier, and there are many more peaks. The peaks are made by re-hooking [I].

> Practical reading: don't spend the first 60 seconds describing the world. Put a character in trouble in the first sentence and explain the world while the trouble is happening.

### 5.2 Open with what and why, not where and when [I]

Traditional order: where, when, who, what, how, why. ("It was a cold night in December. Billy walked down the street. He fell because it was slippery.")

**Modern order: what and why first; who and how second; where and when last.**
1. **What and why it matters** (opt-in).
2. **Who it's for / how we know** (credibility; why trust this).
3. **Where and when** (colour only).

*"Lead with the cake, not the icing."* Every second without a reason to care costs viewers; assume everyone has an invisible timer, and when it reaches zero without a reason to stay, they leave.

Rewrite example (ours): instead of "In 1806, aboard a ship at sea, a boy of thirteen was ordered up the mast", write "A thirteen-year-old was ordered to climb a mast in a storm, and he'd never been this high before. This is 1806, aboard a Royal Navy ship."

**Fairy-tale-style openings** ("Once upon a time, there was a prince, and his kingdom was dying") still work in [C] because they pack stakes into the first lines. The rule is not "never set the scene." It is "never set the scene *instead of* giving a reason to care."

### 5.3 The dance: "but" and "therefore" [A]

Great stories are a dance between **context** and **conflict**. You give a little context, then a conflict appears; a little more context resolves it, then a new conflict appears. Conflicts open loops; context closes them.

**The Parker/Stone rule (paraphrased from a talk the creator cites):** look at your outline beats. If the connector between two beats is "and then", you have something boring. Replace it with **"but"** (a complication) or **"therefore"** (a consequence).

- Bad: "He enters the harbour, and then he meets the captain, and then they eat dinner."
- Good: "He enters the harbour, **but** the captain refuses to see him, **therefore** he must sneak onto the ship, **but** the only unlocked hatch is the cook's storeroom."

The creator points out that one of his best-performing videos has four "but, then" conflict beats in the first 30 seconds.

**Practical use:** write your outline as beats; between every pair, write either "but" or "therefore" out loud. If you can't, the beat doesn't earn its place. Also **write the words in the script** at transitions ("but the real question is...", "so...").

### 5.4 The Addiction Loop: stakes, big question, head fake, re-hook [C]

The most complete single structure in the set. It explains *why* stories hold people.

**The model.** The brain gets its strongest pull not from the reward but from the **prediction** before it. A vending machine is predictable and not addictive. A slot machine is uncertain and addictive. Stories that keep an unresolved prediction running keep people watching. (This is popular science; use it as a craft model.)

**The loop:**

**Step 1: Stakes.** Give the viewer a reason to care, so their mind starts predicting. Three ingredients:
1. **A character to root for** (a person, a team, "you", a client; anything a viewer can picture as a real human).
2. **Something at risk** (the viewer must know what failure looks like).
3. **Urgency** ("by the end of the week" beats "eventually"; "in two hours" beats "later today").
- Stakes can be modest. "48 hours to fix the budget or lose it all" is enough. Stakes simply mean *personally relevant to the ideal viewer*.
- Before the story starts, the viewer should know: what's at risk, who it's happening to, and why now.

**Step 2: The big question.** Give just enough specific information for the viewer to *make a guess*.
- **Weak:** "Something crazy happened." "You won't believe it." (empty teasers, nothing to guess at)
- **Strong:** "Within 30 minutes she said something that completely changed my plan for the next six months." (What did she say? A complaint? A new trend?)
- The sweet spot: enough info to predict, not so much that it overwhelms.
- Every great story has one *massive* big question near the start. The earlier it appears, the longer the viewer stays.
- Tactic: before any important reveal, stop, back up, and add the context that lets the viewer predict.

**Step 3: The head fake.** The outcome breaks the viewer's prediction, but makes sense in hindsight.
- A small surprise gives a small reward; a big one gives a big one; none gives none.
- **Good head fake:** you'd never have guessed it, but as soon as you hear it, you understand why. The clues were there.
- **Cheap surprise:** random, unrelated to the set-up, doesn't make sense. Causes frustration.
- **Process:** (1) work out what a normal viewer would predict from what you've said; (2) deliver something that contradicts it but is clearly logical.
- **The magician analogy:** show the hat, show it is empty, point to the other side, then produce the rabbit. The surprise works because the logic checks out.

**Step 4: The re-hook.** The moment a loop closes, open the next one. No gap.
- A casino tracks *time per hand*; a storyteller must shrink the time between hits.
- Danger zones are the points where one section ends and the next begins. Put a new question, complication, or implication there.
- **Connective phrases that close one loop and open the next in the same breath:** "which would have been great, except...", "but here's the problem with that...", "and that's when I realised...", "which is exactly why...".
- **Relay-race analogy:** the baton must change hands while both runners are moving.

**Worked example (the creator's fairy tale, retold in our words).**
A flat version: *A prince needs a flower from a tower guarded by a dragon. He goes, beats the dragon, gets the flower, saves the kingdom.* Nobody cares; no stakes, no question, no surprise.
The looped version: **Stakes** (a plague, a king begging, a week left, every knight who tried never returned). **Big question** (the dragon is ten times bigger than expected; he has one sword and no armour; how can he possibly pass it?). **Head fake** (the dragon is not guarding the tower; it is chained to it; it is a prisoner; he breaks the chain and it flies away). **Re-hook** (at the top of the tower, next to the flower, stands the king, who sent him; why is he here, and how did he get here first?). The clues for the head fake were planted (the dragon was pulling away, it looked scared), which is why it clicks.

### 5.5 The Dopamine Ladder (six levels) [B]

A checklist for the *viewer's journey*. Aim to take the viewer up all six rungs. The first four belong to the *message* (the video). The last two belong to the *messenger* (you or your channel).

| Level | Name | What happens | Tactic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Stimulation** | The first 1-2 seconds. Colour, motion, brightness grab the eye (the creator says this is processed in about 200 milliseconds) | Strong colour, motion and contrast, different from what the viewer usually sees. Have **your own unique palette and look**; if everyone copies a pattern, it stops working |
| 2 | **Captivation** | An open question forms in the viewer's head (curiosity loop) | Ask: what is the most curiosity-inducing question here, and does it match something my ideal viewer cares about? Either ask it directly, or imply it with details. Use **contrast** (known versus unknown) or a **shocking or weird premise**. A good question is big, non-obvious, and relevant |
| 3 | **Anticipation** | The viewer guesses the answer. Highest reward is *just before* the answer | Give clear, relevant facts that help them guess. Then **head fake** or misdirect to reset the loop, or answer and immediately pose a second question. The facts must be clear: confusing details make people leave |
| 4 | **Validation** | The answer arrives and the loop closes | Reward with something non-obvious. For entertainment: resolve the story. For education: give the concrete value. Do not leave the loop open |
| 5 | **Affection** | The viewer starts to like the creator or character | Likability: presence, vibe, posture, warmth (smiling transmits through the lens), and most strongly, **actually solving their problem**. Don't force it; be authentic |
| 6 | **Revelation** | The viewer realises you are a *reliable source of value* | Deliver non-obvious, differentiated value again and again. When the viewer reacts to your name or face alone, you have built the "Pavlovian" response |

**Notes**
- Levels 5 and 6 are hard for **faceless** channels: "there's nothing to like when there's no personality to attribute trust and taste to" [B]. For us: a **recurring narrator voice and a recurring mascot** (Gizmo on Gizmo Origins) are how an animated channel gets affection.
- Entertainment channels reach level 6 only by being a *consistently* reliable source of entertainment, which is a very high bar.
- Use the ladder as a **review checklist** on any finished video: where does it break?
- Example analysis from [B] (a short stunt video): stimulation = an unusual angle and a slight zoom; captivation = three rapid questions (who are these people, what will we see, will he get caught?); anticipation = he seems stuck, will he be dragged?; validation = he is fine, but the answer is not what you expected (a head-fake and a close at once); affection = it was funny; revelation = needs more videos.

### 5.6 The Story Ladder (six levels) [J]

Another ladder, this time for the *story itself*. Climb one rung at a time. Most people stay on the bottom rungs.

| Level | Name | What it adds | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Reporter** | The Lego bricks: who, what, when, where | Lists facts in order. Flat. "A man stood at the corner. His head was down. He had a bag and a clown mask." Boring because the brain predicts the next fact correctly |
| 2 | **The Illusionist** | **Hooks and re-hooks** (misdirection) | Open a gap where the audience could expect several things. Example: the man stands at the corner, then a car arrives and he hurries in. Four possible futures collapsed into one creates a micro-hook. Aim for a misdirection every 2-3 minutes; a famous film opening has five or six in five minutes |
| 3 | **The Champion** | **Stakes** (a reason for this viewer to care) | Educational: state the pain directly. Entertainment: build a character or conflict the viewer can see themselves in. "Aim the cannon" at your specific viewer |
| 4 | **The Architect** | **Arcs and loops** | Arcs: tension rises and falls (choppier sentences when building, relaxed when releasing). Modern internet stories have several fast arcs. For educational videos use **loops** that close (see 5.8). Adds *navigation*: at any moment the viewer knows where they are |
| 5 | **The Translator** | **Resonance / comprehension** | Second pass through the script: does every line land? Use on-target visuals. Without visuals, add a **second explanation with a simpler metaphor**. Ask "how might a novice be confused here?" |
| 6 | **The Maestro** | **Signature style** | The thing only you do: a way of explaining, a visual style, a rhythm. Appears only after many stories. Master levels 1-5 first |

**Core insight at the bottom of the ladder:** if you plot a viewer's base attention it is a flat line, and flat lines are boring. A great story **bends the line**: it keeps the brain slightly off balance, again and again [J].

### 5.7 Re-hooks and loop openers [H][I][C]

Three sources describe the same technique from different angles.

- **Re-hooking / re-buy-in [I].** Like a poker player who loses but re-buys and stays at the table: after the first loop closes, tee up *new stakes or a new pain* so the viewer re-buys. Example: you give the fix for the creative; then say "but even great creative gets wasted unless you solve this". You solved one problem and created the next one, so they stay for the next.
- **Loop openers [H].** Short phrases that reflip the attention hourglass. Every viewer has a sand timer. A hook flips it; after 20-30 seconds in short-form, or one to two minutes in long-form, it runs out and viewers leave. Drop a loop opener at each major transition.
- **The re-hook beat [C].** Close a loop and open another in the same sentence, with no dead air.

**Loop-opener rhythm:** confirm the last point was valuable, then tease that the next one is *even more* valuable for a different reason. Almost all use a contrast word.
- "That works. But that's not even the interesting part."
- "Most people stop here. But here's what nobody mentions."
- "Okay, that was big. The next one matters even more."
- "And this is where it gets strange."

**How many?** A video with five main sections wants three or four loop openers [H]. Cadence: roughly every 20-30 seconds in short-form, every 60-90 seconds in long-form [H].

**Result to look for:** the retention graph stops decaying in the middle and stays roughly flat [H].

### 5.8 Arcs, loops and story navigation [J]

- **Arcs** go up and down. Build tension with shorter, choppier sentences, rising action, conflict; release tension with falling action, relaxation. The classic arc is one wave, but online stories use several quick waves around different hooks. When two characters are at different stages of their own arcs, the contrast adds depth.
- **Loops** *close*. In informational videos: one major loop (the whole video) and several minor loops (each section). A repeatable pattern inside each minor loop trains the viewer to know when the next loop is about to open. The creator's own pattern: *what's going on, why it matters, an example, what to do about it.*
- **Story navigation.** At any point, the viewer should be able to say where they are in the story. If you pause anywhere, it should be clear. A repeatable section pattern makes this automatic.
- **For narrative stories (ours):** use the arc idea (each chapter has its own mini rise and fall) *and* the loop idea (each chapter opens a question and closes it). For an episodic series, every episode closes its own loop and opens a series-level loop (see 12).

### 5.9 Endings [A][B][C]

1. **Plan the ending first** and write backwards. The creator writes the first and last lines, leaves space between, then fills the middle [A]. He cites film directors who must know the ending before building a complicated middle.
2. **The last dab.** The final line should be so memorable that if it were the only thing heard, someone would share it [A].
3. **Make the last line set up the first** when the video may loop (short-form replay) [A]. The ninth hitter sets the table for the top of the order.
4. **Close the loop.** For entertainment, resolve the story; don't end on a cliffhanger. For education, give the promised payoff [B].
5. **Exception, series:** a deliberate cliffhanger works if the *episode's* loop is closed and a *series-level* loop is opened. See 12.
6. **Re-hook at the end** into the next video or part (the creator ends by pointing to a related video).

## 6. CHARACTERS AND STAKES

**Hero versus villain [I].** A hero alone is cool; hero versus villain is a blockbuster because the distance between hero and villain is greater than hero versus nothing. This is contrast. The villain doesn't have to be a person: it can be a system, a rival method, the clock, the weather, the thing everyone believes. Name the opposition and frame it ("they do this, we do that").

**Something to root for [I][J].** The more likable the hero or relatable the situation, the more viewers invest. They should see *themselves* as the protagonist, either through a character or through a movement. How: build common ground, show the hero's reason, let the viewer see themselves in the situation. The creator illustrates this with a sports-film scene where a coach demands loyalty to the team over individual glory; the viewer who has played sport sees themselves in the players.

**Stakes by genre [C][J][G].**
- **Educational:** the stakes are the outcome the viewer wants (more money, time, skills).
- **Entertainment:** the stakes are a curiosity-inducing scenario: what will happen to this character?
- **Stakes need personal relevance,** not size. "My paid budget will be gone in 48 hours" is enough to a business owner.

**Identification, not perfection.** The viewer must see a person in a real situation. Give the character a want, a risk and a clock in the first lines.

**Affection and the recurring face [B].** Liking comes from a consistent personality. For us, this means recurring characters and a recognisable narrator voice.

**The "something to lose" test:** After the first 15 seconds, can you answer: who is it, what do they want, what might they lose, and by when? If not, add that line before anything else.

## 7. LANGUAGE AND DELIVERY

### 7.1 The six story locks [H]

Word-level tricks you can apply to any finished script by swapping a few words. The creator calls them "story locks" because each one locks attention in place. The deepest one (6) powers the others.

**Lock 1: Term branding.** Give your concept a name.
- Why: once something has a name, it feels important and the viewer wants to know what it means. A name they do not recognise creates instant anticipation ("what is that?") that holds them like quicksand until you explain it. This is the labelling effect.
- How: before publishing, look at the core idea and ask "could I package this and name it?" Not everything needs a name; your contrarian ideas and best frameworks do.
- Our example: instead of "the way sailors were punished by being made to hang on in storms", call it **"the Watch Below"**; instead of "a rule where junior boys clean first", call it **"the Powder Monkey Rule"**.
- Note: the creator names his own ideas relentlessly (story locks, dopamine ladder, lock-in zone). That is a signature move; it also makes the material memorable.

**Lock 2: Embedded truths.** State things as if they are already true.
- Why: "If you try this" gives the viewer a fork in the road to decide whether it's for them. "When you try this" has only one road. Each hedge (if, maybe, might, could, probably) is a tiny exit door for attention.
- Swaps: "if you try" becomes "when you try"; "this might work because" becomes "the reason this works"; "you might notice" becomes "once you see it, you can't unsee it"; "some creators might be making a mistake" becomes "the mistake most creators make is..."
- Tactic: go through the script and remove non-committal words; if it sounds like hedging, state it plainly. (Keep honesty: only state things you actually know; don't invent certainty.)

**Lock 3: Thought narration.** Say what the viewer is thinking, as they think it.
- Why: it feels like mind-reading, builds trust ("they get me"), and forces them to stay for your answer. It turns one script into a simulated one-to-one conversation (personalisation at scale) and breaks the fourth wall to refocus attention.
- Phrases: "Now the question you're probably thinking is...", "If I were you, the thing on my mind right now would be...", "You're probably worried about...".
- Tactic: after each major point, ask "what should the viewer be thinking right now?" and say it. A few times per video is enough.
- Our example: "Now you're thinking, 'A horse that does math? How?' Exactly."

**Lock 4: Negative frames.** Flip the positive advice into a warning.
- Why: the brain prioritises threats over opportunities (loss aversion; the source says people are about twice as motivated to avoid pain as to seek gain; treat the number as rough). A warning makes the viewer ask "am I doing that?"
- Examples: "Here's how to build a personal brand" becomes "The worst thing you can do for your personal brand is this". A triple "never, ever, ever" locks on to a warning.
- **The negative flip:** "Try posting more consistently" becomes "This posting mistake is costing you followers."
- Do it sometimes, not always. A softer version ("you're making this harder than it needs to be") still works.
- For story: "Don't open that door" has more pull than "open the door."

**Lock 5: Loop openers.** See section 5.7.
- Every viewer's attention is an hourglass. A hook flips it; the sand runs out after 20-30 seconds if you only deliver facts. A loop opener flips it again by confirming the last point and teasing a bigger next one, usually with a contrast word.

**Lock 6: Contrast words.** The engine under all the others.
- Curiosity comes from a gap between what someone expects and what you believe is true. The bigger the gap, the more curiosity. Contrast words build the gap.
- **But** is the strongest. A but B; "doing A, but also B"; "you might think A, but actually B".
- Others: **actually** (what you thought was wrong), **instead** (a redirect), **turns out** ("it turns out the algorithm doesn't care"), **except** (an exception to the rule), **yet** (a contradiction; old-fashioned).
- Why it's the foundation: term branding contrasts with what they expect to hear; embedded truths contrast with uncertainty; thought narration contrasts with expecting to be talked at; negative frames contrast with expected advice; loop openers contrast with what you just covered.
- Tactic: take each sentence that makes a clean point and split it in half, leaning one way and then the other. **Do not overuse** "but" in every sentence; it becomes friction-heavy.

### 7.2 Rhythm [A]

The brain finds natural pacing soothing. Monotony makes people leave.
- A passage of same-length sentences droning on reads like a stuck record. The source reads aloud a famous passage on this (a writing teacher's demonstration): five-word sentences are fine, but several in a row become monotonous; vary short, medium, and occasionally a long sentence that builds like a crescendo.
- **Test:** write every sentence on a separate line. If the right-hand edge is jagged, the lengths vary. If lines all end at about the same point, the rhythm is flat.
- Short sentences in the hook (staccato) for clarity and density [E]; longer ones later.
- Our addition: **read it aloud, and for animation, mark the beat where the cut or gag lands.** Rhythm applies to sound and edits too.

### 7.3 Tone [A]

The best creators sound conversational, as if you're in the room with them. The conscious barrier ("am I being sold to?") drops and you simply feel part of a conversation.
- Tactic: write and record as if talking to **one close friend**. Print their picture and tape it under the lens, or write it as a text or voice note to them. Over time the camera stops being a camera.
- Early videos often sound like talking *at* people; the fix is reps and writing to one person.
- For narration: use "you", contractions, short asides, and the occasional direct question.

### 7.4 Clarity [D][G]

- Fewer words, but never so few that it can be misunderstood.
- Simple words, ideally sixth-grade reading level.
- Active voice.
- Read the hook alone: can it be read two ways?
- No clever wordplay in the hook.
- If you say a complex thing, **say it twice**: once like to a colleague, then again with a metaphor like to a child [I]. A secondary metaphor gives two shots at comprehension [J].

### 7.5 Atomic sharability [I]

The ability to condense a story to a unit so small a viewer can retell it after one watch (the source's example: a famous four-word warning shouted on a night ride). Cut the fluff, use simple words and ideas, punchy sentences. Write so that others could retell it.
- Test: after drafting, can you say the story in one sentence a friend would repeat? If not, simplify.

### 7.6 Story lenses [A]

A topic alone is not enough; dozens of creators cover the same one. A **story lens** is your unique angle or spin, like a prism in front of a beam of light.
- Process: list the common angles on a topic; list the less common; choose one almost nobody has.
- Example in the source: for a famous musician attending a big sports game, common lenses are what she wore and how she reacted; a rarer lens is a prediction; the rarest is the business impact on the league. The rare lens became a million-view video.
- For us: pick the angle nobody else uses (the invention told from the failed experiment, the gadget's first owner, the person who got it wrong).

### 7.7 Signature style [J]

The final 1%: a one-of-one thing others cannot copy (a way of explaining, a visual palette, a delivery). It takes hundreds of reps to discover, so master levels 1-5 first; the signature appears with volume. For us the signature is the Beanfolk 3D look (round bean people, drawn faces), word-timed pop-ins and whips, sound on every beat, and loops back to line one.

### 7.8 Specificity and power words [G]

- Specific numbers beat vague ones ("22 days", "$2,700").
- Compress sentences into vivid two- to three-word phrases ("heat-seeking missiles", "social media machine").
- Names and terms (especially capitalised on screen) attract the eye.

## 8. VISUAL STORYTELLING

All the sources agree: **pictures lead, words confirm.**

1. **Visual hooks beat spoken hooks by a large margin** (the creator says "10 times" in one video and "100 times" in another; treat as "much more") because eyes perceive faster than ears [A][E][F].
2. **Visual, speech, visual** is how viewers take in a clip: image first, then the words, then back to the image to check [F]. Design for that sandwich.
3. **Put text on screen** in a big bold font: three to five words that distil what you are saying [E].
4. **Motion has a sweet spot:** enough to catch the eye (like an animal noticing movement out of the corner of its eye), not so much it overwhelms; too little bores [B][E].
5. **Colour, motion, brightness, contrast** drive the first reaction. Use a palette and look that is unique to you, because patterns that everyone copies stop working [B].
6. **Align every layer** (see 4.5). Misaligned visuals cause comprehension loss [F].
7. **Show, don't make them imagine.** If you don't give a visual, the viewer must build the picture from words alone, and that often causes confusion. "A picture's worth a thousand words; a video's worth a thousand pictures" [I][J].
8. **Five categories of visual hook** [G]: (1) graphic and text overlays; (2) pattern interrupts or visual switching; (3) subject motion (moving in or out of frame); (4) visual effects or transitions; (5) visual selection (choosing a striking image).
   - Easiest three to start: move the subject in the frame, move the visual in the frame, move text over the visual [G].
   - Don't overdo it; visual hooks can feel gimmicky if forced [G].
9. **Visual pacifier** (a small repeating activity that holds subconscious attention) [F].
10. **Use visuals to explain the unseeable** (the source cites a film that visualised an impossible place so viewers could grasp it) [J].
11. **Quality of packaging builds trust:** polish, framing, sound, lighting. Care is read as trustworthiness [G].
12. **Hire or build strength in the visual craft:** the creator credits his editors for much of the visual quality [I].

but, however, yet, although, instead, actually, turns out, except, until, unless, even though, despite, then suddenly, which was fine, except...

### 10.4 Head-fake builders (for stories)

- **Reverse the villain:** the monster is the prisoner (the source's chained dragon).
- **Reverse the helper:** the person helping is the cause.
- **Reverse the scale:** the big threat is a distraction; the small detail is the danger.
- **Reverse the timing:** it already happened; they are just now noticing.
- **Reverse the motive:** they did it for the opposite reason.
- **Rule:** plant at least two clues earlier that make the reverse feel inevitable once revealed.

### 10.5 Negative-flip examples

| Positive | Negative flip |
|---|---|
| How to survive your first night aboard | The mistake that gets new sailors punished on night one |
| Three tips for climbing the mast | The one thing that kills climbers, and nobody warns you |
| How to win the captain's trust | Never do this in front of the captain |

### 10.6 Thought-narration bank

- "Now you're thinking: why would anyone do that?"
- "I know what you're asking: is this real?"
- "The obvious question is: who paid for all this?"
- "If you're anything like me, you're worried about [X]."

### 10.7 Script skeleton: 8-10 minute story video (long-form reference)

| Time | Block | What to include |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00-0:05 | **Hook (key visual + 3-step formula)** | Strongest image, one-line premise, contrast |
| 0:05-0:20 | **Lock-in zone** | Confirm the promise; introduce the character; a concrete fact or gag; show the style |
| 0:20-1:00 | **Stakes + big question** | Who, what's at risk, by when; the question the viewer will guess at |
| 1:00-2:30 | **Act 1 beats ("but/therefore")** | First head fake; end with a re-hook |
| 2:30-4:00 | **Act 2 beats** | Complications; a gag release; second head fake; re-hook |
| 4:00-6:30 | **Act 3 beats** | Biggest conflict; deepen the emotion; maybe a false win; re-hook |
| 6:30-8:30 | **Climax and resolution** | Pay off the big question; close the main loop |
| 8:30-9:30 | **Last dab and next-loop** | Memorable final line; open the series-level loop or point to the next video |

Re-hooks about every 60-90 seconds; misdirection about every 2-3 minutes; a release beat (humour or calm) after every big peak.

### 10.8 Script skeleton: 60-second short

| Seconds | Block |
|---|---|
| 0-3 | Hook: image + text + one line |
| 3-8 | Lock-in: confirm, character, first fact |
| 8-25 | Stakes + question + first head fake |
| 25-45 | Escalation + re-hook |
| 45-58 | Payoff + last dab |
| 58-60 | Loop back to the first line |

## 11. Worked examples

*All story content in the examples below is invented to show structure. Made-up stories are fine (our default); only a true story needs its facts checked.*

### 11.1 Flat versus looped (original, in our style)

**Flat (vending-machine version).**
*A boy joins a ship. He is scared. He works hard. He becomes an officer.*
No stakes, no question, no surprise.

**Looped version.**
- **Stakes.** *Thirteen-year-old Tom has three days to learn every rope on a warship, or the bosun will send him home in disgrace, and his family has nothing left.* (character, risk, clock)
- **Big question.** *The bosun says the ship has 200 ropes, and each has a name. Tom can read one word.* How is he going to learn 200 names in three days?
- **Head fake.** *Tom does not memorise a single rope. He draws a map of the ship and gives each rope a place in a song. By day two the whole crew is humming it, and the bosun can't get it out of his head.* (The viewer predicted cramming; the answer is a mnemonic. Clues planted earlier: Tom's mother sang to him; he was bad at reading but good at remembering tunes.)
- **Re-hook.** *Which would have been the happy ending, except the captain has heard the song too, and he wants to know who taught it to the crew.*

### 11.2 Hook rewrites

| Weak hook | Why it fails | Rewrite |
|---|---|---|
| "You won't believe what I found on the ship." | Delay, no topic | "A 200-year-old ship's log says the cook was paid more than the doctor." |
| "I'm going to tell you a story about hats." | No gap, no stakes | "This hat cost the captain his dinner, and the boy who lost it three days to find it." |
| "Today we're talking about Navy ranks." | Topic yes, no gap, no you | "If you joined the navy at thirteen, there were ten ranks above you, and one way to skip nine." |
| "Sailors had it tough." | Vague | "Sailors drank four pints of beer a day, and nobody called it a problem." |

### 11.3 The 3-step formula applied

> **Context lean:** The Royal Navy gave every sailor a daily rum ration. **Scroll-stop:** But the rum was never the real reward. **Snapback:** The real reward was the one hour a day the captain was not allowed to give orders.

(Treat all content here as an illustration of structure; a made-up story only has to make sense inside its own world.)

### 11.4 Dopamine-ladder review of a scene (checklist use)

For any finished 20-second clip, mark each rung:
1. Stimulation: what's the first-frame hit? 2. Captivation: what question appears? 3. Anticipation: what do they guess? 4. Validation: when is it answered? 5. Affection: who do they like? 6. Revelation: why come back?
If a rung is empty, add a beat.

## 12. How this applies to our Shorts (Gizmo Origins, Beanfolk)

*This section is our own interpretation, not from the sources. The full rulebook for our Shorts is `knowledge/SHORTS_PLAYBOOK.md`.*

**Our format:** 30-70 second vertical Shorts (1080x1920) in the Beanfolk 3D style: round bean people with drawn-on faces in cosy 3D
sets, a fast narrator (about 3.5-4 words a second), captions one or two words at a time, a new picture every 1-1.5 seconds, whips
and pop-ins on words, sound on every beat. Stories are original and made-up by default (true only when the owner asks): a strange claim, doubt, clues, "Meanwhile", the
"You see..." reveal, a payoff, and a last line that loops into the first. Gizmo Origins tells surprising stories behind everyday
gadgets; Beanfolk tells strange stories with a twist.

### 12.1 Mapping the rules to our toolkit

| Rule | What we do in our pipeline |
|---|---|
| Key visual first [F] | Frame 1 already shows the strangest thing in the story (the melting chocolate, the heart-beating circuit), in motion |
| Stimulation [B] | Bright palette, a moving camera from frame 1, a pop-in year or stamp in the first second |
| Text hook [G] | A pop-in of 2-4 words (subject plus gap, no punctuation) on the hook's key word |
| Alignment [F][G] | Line 1, the picture, the pop-in and the sound all say the same thing in the first two seconds |
| Speed to value [D] | No intro, no logo; the strange part lands inside the first ~2 seconds |
| Context lean / but / snapback [E] | Line 1 states the fact; the next line turns it with "But"; the picture whips on "But" |
| Stakes [C] | A named character (invented is fine), what could go wrong, a number or a time on screen |
| Big question [C] | Clues the viewer can guess from (the room wasn't hot; the hoof stops on the right number) |
| Head fake [C] | The "You see..." reveal: surprising, but every clue was shown earlier |
| Re-hook [C][H] | A loop-opener line every 15-20 seconds ("Meanwhile...", "But then...", "And that's the strange part.") with a whip and a whoosh |
| Release beats [I] | A gag after each peak (the egg in the coworker's face, the proud snort) |
| Affection [B] | One narrator voice on every Short; Gizmo the mascot cameos on Gizmo Origins |
| Last dab [A] | A short punchy payoff right after the reveal, then the loop line (8 seconds at most after the reveal) |
| Comments | An on-screen question pop-in in the last 3-4 seconds (never spoken) and a pinned comment on every upload |

### 12.2 What our first analytics said (2026-11-11)

About 75% of viewers stayed past the swipe and watched about 80% of each Short: hooks and pacing work. Viewers left once the secret was
out (a 17-second tail after the reveal), and nobody commented. Hence the late reveal, the 8-second tail and the comment question
(`docs/lessons/analytics-retention-comments.md`).

### 12.3 Pre-flight questions for any new Short

1. What is the key visual for the first second?
2. What is the craziest sentence in this story, and can it be line 1?
3. Who is the character, what happened to them, and what specific detail (a name, a time, a number) makes it vivid?
4. What will the viewer guess, and what is the head fake? Which two clues are planted?
5. Where are the re-hooks (one every 15-20 seconds)?
6. Does the reveal end at 80-85% of the runtime, with at most 8 seconds after it?
7. What is the last line, and does it flow into line 1?
8. What is the comment question?

## 13. Common mistakes (diagnosis table)

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix | Section |
|---|---|---|---|
| Viewers leave in the first 5 seconds | Delay, vague teaser, no visual | Topic in sentence one; stronger key visual | 4.2 |
| Viewers leave around 20-30 seconds | No lock-in, no proof, no re-hook | Confirm the promise; add proof; loop opener | 4.9, 5.7 |
| Retention decays steadily through the middle | Facts without re-hooks | Add a loop opener every 60-90 s; open new stakes | 5.7 |
| Viewers say "I got confused" | Jargon, misaligned layers, passive voice | Simplify; align picture, words, text | 4.2, 4.5 |
| Viewers say "boring" | "And then" structure; flat intensity | Replace with but/therefore; start high | 5.1, 5.3 |
| Twist feels cheap | No clues planted | Add two earlier clues | 5.4 |
| Story ends flat | No planned ending; open loops | Write the last line first; close loops | 5.9 |
| Viewers don't care about the hero | No stakes or no relatable want | Add character, risk, clock | 5.4, 6 |
| Good retention, no sharing | Idea too big or vague | Cut to one retellable sentence | 7.5 |
| Everything sounds the same | No lens, no signature | Choose a rarer angle; develop signature | 7.6, 7.7 |
| Hooks sound like ads | Hedging, "I" language | Use you/your, embedded truths | 4.2, 7.1 |
| Visuals fight the narration | No key visual process | Choose the visual first | 4.6 |

## 14. Master checklists

### 14.1 Before writing
- [ ] One-sentence premise a friend would repeat
- [ ] Key visual for seconds 0-3
- [ ] Character, want, risk, clock
- [ ] Villain defined
- [ ] The last line written
- [ ] A rare story lens chosen
- [ ] Big question defined
- [ ] Head fake with two clues defined

### 14.2 Hook
(See 4.10.)

### 14.3 Middle
- [ ] Every beat is linked with but or therefore
- [ ] A loop opener at every section break
- [ ] A peak and release rhythm (peak every 2-5 minutes)
- [ ] A release beat (gag) after each peak
- [ ] A misdirection every 2-3 minutes
- [ ] Facts arrive only when the viewer can use them to guess
- [ ] No more than one new concept at a time

### 14.4 Ending
- [ ] The main loop is closed
- [ ] The last line is memorable and shareable
- [ ] A series-level loop (or next video) opens
- [ ] The last line connects to the first (for replay)

### 14.5 Polish
- [ ] Sentence-length variety (jagged edge)
- [ ] Hedges removed (if, maybe, might)
- [ ] Contrast words placed on major points
- [ ] A few thought-narration lines
- [ ] Key ideas named
- [ ] Complex ideas said twice (colleague, child)
- [ ] Visuals match the words on every line
- [ ] Any moment where a novice might be confused is fixed
- [ ] Two clues planted before the twist (and, only for a true story, facts checked)

## 15. Glossary

- **Atomic sharability:** a story reduced to a unit small enough to retell after one watch.
- **Big question:** a specific question the viewer can guess at.
- **Context lean:** hook step 1; clear topic plus something that makes the viewer lean in.
- **Contrarian snapback:** hook step 3; a sentence that reverses the lean.
- **Contrast:** the distance between what the viewer believes (A) and what you show (B).
- **Culture hopping:** wrapping an unfamiliar idea in a familiar reference.
- **Curiosity loop:** repeated cycles of question, partial answer, new question.
- **Dance (the):** alternation of context and conflict.
- **Delay:** hook mistake; topic arrives late.
- **Embedded truth:** a statement framed as already true.
- **Freeze point:** the moment a scrolling viewer stops.
- **Head fake:** a reveal that breaks the viewer's prediction but makes sense.
- **Interrupt theory:** hooks work by triggering an orienting response (a "wait, what was that?" reaction).
- **Key visual:** the single strongest image for the first seconds.
- **Labelling effect:** named things feel more important.
- **Lock-in zone:** the seconds after the hook where trust is earned.
- **Loop opener:** a phrase that closes a loop and opens another.
- **Loss aversion:** people react more strongly to avoiding loss than to gaining.
- **Negative flip:** turning advice into a warning.
- **Orienting response:** an involuntary reaction to a new stimulus.
- **Power words:** short vivid phrases that amplify an outcome or pain.
- **Re-hook / re-buy-in:** a new question or stake after the old one closes.
- **Scroll-stop interjection:** hook step 2; a short contrast line.
- **Speed to value:** how fast the viewer reaches clarity or value.
- **Staccato:** short sentences.
- **Stakes:** character plus risk plus urgency.
- **Story lens:** your unique angle on a topic.
- **Story locks:** six word-level tricks for locking attention.
- **Text hook:** on-screen title text distinct from captions.
- **Thought narration:** saying what the viewer is thinking.
- **Visual pacifier:** background activity that holds subconscious attention.

## 16. Where the sources agree, and where they differ

**Strong agreement (appears in many videos):**
- Contrast creates curiosity. [D][E][F][H][J]
- Re-hook constantly. [C][H][I][J]
- The picture leads; align picture, words, text. [A][E][F][G]
- Clarity beats cleverness. [D][G]
- Start with what and why. [D][I]
- Stakes need a character, a risk and a clock. [C][J]

**Differences and tensions:**
- **Vague hooks.** [D] says vague hooks work less often and only because of face, emotion or text; [A] says opaque lines are fatal. Consistent: avoid them.
- **Opening with the scene.** [I] says avoid where/when openers; [C]'s fairy tale opens "Once upon a time". Resolved in 5.2: scene is fine if the stakes arrive in the first lines.
- **Speed versus stakes.** [D][G] push for minimal words; [C] says stakes need a few sentences. Resolution: compress both; "short" and "complete" can coexist.
- **Visual importance.** [E] says visual hooks matter 100 times more than words; [G] says visual hooks are the least important of the three layers once text and speech are strong ("yappers" prove it). Both can be true: visuals matter most for a *silent glance*; text and speech carry weight when someone is already looking.
- **Cliffhangers.** [B] says do not end on a cliffhanger (entertainment); for our Shorts, close the loop and let the last line run back into the first (section 12).
- **Hedging and honesty.** [H] says remove hedges; honesty requires that you only state what you know.

**What the sources do not cover (gaps):**
- Series and multi-episode structure.
- Animation, sound design, and music as storytelling tools.
- Emotional storytelling beyond curiosity (grief, joy, awe).
- Humour construction.
- Ethics of clickbait versus honest hooks (touched only by "pay it off").
- Measuring and iterating (retention graph analysis is only mentioned).
