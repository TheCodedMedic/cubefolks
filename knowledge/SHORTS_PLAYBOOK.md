# Shorts Playbook: hooks and story for animated Shorts of any length (about 20-60 seconds)

The rulebook for every Short on the owner's channels (Gizmo Origins: surprising stories behind everyday gadgets; Beanfolk: strange
stories with a twist). **Stories are original and made-up by default** (the owner's rule, 2026-11-11, in `CLAUDE.md`); a true story only
when the owner asks or brings one. Built from `knowledge/STORYTELLING_HOOKS_GUIDE.md` (the source rules, tagged [A]-[J]), the craft notes in
`knowledge/craft/`, and our own numbers (`docs/lessons/analytics-retention-comments.md`). **Every Short's script and first second are
checked against this file before voicing and again before rendering.**

The whole craft in one line: *stop the thumb with the strangest thing in the story, give the viewer a question they can guess at, keep
reopening it, answer late with a surprise that was fair all along, then loop.*

---

## 1. The 1-second hook checklist

Run it on line 1 and frame 1, in this order. Every box must be ticked.

**The words**
- [ ] **The craziest sentence in the story is line 1.** Not the background, not the year alone: the impossible-sounding claim,
      stated flat, as if it is simply true ("a chocolate bar melted in his pocket, and it gave the world the microwave").
- [ ] **Don't open with the title** (no "What if you never slept again?" as line 1) and **don't open with a year or a time-passing
      phrase** ("In 1964...", "Back in...", "One day..."): it is repetitive across our Shorts (the owner, 2026-11-11). Open on the
      strange thing itself ("A kid stayed awake for eleven days straight, and he did it for a science fair."). Years go on screen
      as a small pop-in, or later in the narration if they matter. Vary the opening shape from Short to Short.
- [ ] **The strange part lands inside about 2 seconds** (the first 5-8 words). No warm-up, no "you won't believe this", no "this is crazy" [D].
- [ ] **One meaning only.** Short, everyday words; active voice; a 10-year-old gets it on one hearing [D][G].
- [ ] **Contrast is built in:** what anyone expects (A) against what happened (B) inside the first one or two lines [D][E][F].
- [ ] **Specific:** a name, a place, a time, a number (invented ones are fine: "Mr. Pell", "3:12 a.m.", "forty-one keys") [G].
- [ ] **10-16 words** for line 1; staccato. Long sentences come later [E].
- [ ] **Certain, never hedged:** no "maybe", "might", "could"; "the reason" not "one possible reason" [H]. (For a true story, keep its facts accurate.)
- [ ] **The Short pays it off.** A made-up story only has to make sense inside its own world; a hook the video doesn't keep is
      clickbait, and viewers leave and don't come back [E].

**The picture, text and sound**
- [ ] **Frame 1 already shows the strange thing, in motion** (the melting chocolate, the pulsing circuit, the horse at the chalkboard),
      with the camera moving. No title card, no logo, no establishing shot first [F][B].
- [ ] **Frame 0 already tells the hook** (vidIQ, 2026-11-11): the hook pop-in is fully on screen from the first frame (no slow
      build or fade-in), the character is already mid-reaction, the camera already moving. No 1.5 s lead-in.
- [ ] **A 2-4 word pop-in** lands on the hook's key word: subject plus gap ("1945", "NO DIPLOMA", "1¢ PER TAIL"), no punctuation,
      on screen about 2-3 s, never over a face [G].
- [ ] **A sound on frame 1** (a whoosh, a pop, the room tone): never silence.
- [ ] **All layers say the same thing:** mute it and you can guess the words; cover the picture and you can guess the image [F][G].
- [ ] **Title and thumbnail promise the same thing** as line 1 and frame 1.

**The next 4 seconds (the lock-in zone [G])**
- [ ] Confirms the hook (no bait and switch), gives proof (who, when, a concrete detail) and shows care (a polished, busy frame).

If the story has no crazy first line and no strong first image, **pick another story** [F].

## 2. The story shape for a Short

**Length is whatever the story needs (the owner, 2026-11-11): anywhere from about 20 to 60 seconds.** A tight 30-second Short that
holds everyone beats a padded minute. Never stretch a story to reach a length, and never cut a great beat just to get shorter. At
3.5-4 words a second: 20 s ≈ 70-80 words, 30 s ≈ 105-120, 45 s ≈ 160-180, 60 s ≈ 210-230.

A Short is one big question, asked in second 1 and answered late. The percentages are what matter; the seconds below are for a
60 s Short. **Shorter Shorts drop beats, not the shape:** a 20-30 s Short is hook, lock-in, one doubt beat with a clue, the reveal,
the payoff and loop (no Meanwhile); a 40-45 s Short keeps one re-hook and the Meanwhile.

| Time | % | Beat | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-2 s | 0-3% | **Hook** | The craziest line + the key visual + a pop-in |
| 2-8 s | 3-12% | **Lock-in** | Who, where, when, one concrete detail; the first gag |
| 8-20 s | 12-30% | **Doubt and stakes** | People laugh, experts come, something is at risk; clues the viewer can guess from |
| ~18 s | ~30% | **Re-hook 1** | "But then..." / "And that's when it got strange." |
| 20-35 s | 30-55% | **Escalation** | Each beat raises it ("but" / "therefore", never "and then"); a gag after each peak |
| ~30-38 s | ~50-60% | **Meanwhile** | A parallel mystery or second clue that narrows the guess (re-hook 2) |
| 38-45 s | 60-70% | **The last clue** | The detail that makes the answer feel close (re-hook 3) |
| 45-55 s | 70-85% | **"You see..." reveal** | The hidden mechanism, simply, with one picture that explains it; surprising but every clue was shown |
| last ≤8 s | 85-100% | **Payoff + loop** | One punchy line that lands the point as a complete sentence, then the loop line into line 1; the comment question pops up on screen in the last 3-4 s |

Rules:
1. **One story, one question.** Everything before the reveal makes the question sharper; nothing wanders.
2. **"But" or "therefore" between every two beats.** If you can only say "and then", cut or merge the beat [A].
3. **Plant at least two clues** before the reveal so it feels inevitable in hindsight (the hoof stops on the right number; the room wasn't hot) [C].
4. **The reveal ends at 80-85% of the runtime.** People leave once the secret is out: **at most 8 seconds after it.** No summary, no moral, no "and today...".
5. **Care before you hurt:** a beat of warmth or a gag with the person before the trouble [craft 01].
6. **Name the idea** on screen when it appears ("CLEVER HANS EFFECT", "PERVERSE INCENTIVE") [H].
7. **Say a hard idea twice:** once plainly, once as a picture or a simple comparison [I][J].
8. Words to match the story's length (20 s ≈ 75, 30 s ≈ 110, 45 s ≈ 170, 60 s ≈ 220); one sentence per beat; 3.5-4 words a second.

## 3. Re-hooks and loop endings

**Re-hooks.** The attention timer runs out every 15-20 seconds in a Short. Flip it with a loop opener at each section change, on a
whip and a whoosh, so a new question opens in the same breath an old one closes [C][H].
- "But here's the strange part."
- "That should have been the end. It wasn't."
- "And then someone noticed something odd."
- "Meanwhile, ..."
- "Which would have been fine, except..."
- "So he tried something even stranger."
- "But nobody could explain one thing."
- Thought narration, once per Short at most: "Now you're thinking: how could a horse do math?" [H]

**Head fakes** (the "You see..." twist) [C]: work out what a viewer would guess from the clues, then reveal something different that
the same clues prove. Reverse the helper (the watchers were giving the answers), the motive (they weren't killing rats, they were
farming them), or the scale (the snack was the experiment).

**Loop endings.**
1. Land the point first, as a complete sentence ("He was a genius at reading people."). A bare hanging fragment sounds unfinished.
2. Then the loop line, which runs grammatically into line 1 ("So no one can officially beat the time..." → "a kid stayed awake for eleven days straight...").
3. The last shot cuts straight into the first shot (same set or same camera), so a replay feels seamless.
4. **A comment question pops up on screen in the last 3-4 seconds** (never spoken; a spoken question breaks the loop): "Did YOU
   guess the trick? 👇", "Hunt the rats or farm them? 👇". Write a matching **pinned comment** with the title.

## 4. Caption, visual and pacing rules

**Captions**
- One or two words at a time, from the measured word times; heavy rounded white type with a dark outline, at about 60% of the height,
  a tiny pop on each word; a highlight colour on key words (names, numbers, the twist word).
- Pop-in text (not captions) is the text hook: 2-4 words, no punctuation, never over a face, never two at once in the same place.

**Visuals**
- Pictures lead, words confirm: every line has a picture that shows exactly what is said [F][I].
- A new picture every 1-1.5 seconds; every camera moves (push, orbit, crane, tilt); most changes are whips with a whoosh.
- Faces change on the words; characters act (gasp, point, laugh, shrug). Bright, warm palette; the reveal in a deep violet grade.
- The owner's picture notes: nothing too close to the lens; no back of a head (or hat) filling the frame; faces bright in dark
  grades; approved proportions on every new character; no blurry foreground opening a shot.
- One idea per picture. Explain the unseeable with a simple cartoon (spinning water molecules, a spark that can't get through).

**Pacing and sound**
- The narrator at 3.5-4 words a second, gaps about 0.15 s; speed up slow lines rather than cutting the gaps.
- Sound on every beat: a whoosh on every whip, a pop on every pop-in, real reactions (laughs, cheers, gasps, the animal's voice).
- Music 15 dB under the voice (measured). A beat of near-silence just before the reveal is a strong pattern break.
- Vary sentence length: staccato in the hook, longer in the middle, short again at the payoff [A].

## 5. Ten hook templates (with examples in our style)

The examples happen to be true stories we made; the same templates work for made-up stories (the default). Only a true story needs
accurate facts.

| # | Template | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Accident origin:** "[Small accident], and it gave the world [everyday thing]." | "A chocolate bar melted in a man's pocket, and it gave the world the microwave." |
| 2 | **One mistake, huge result:** "[Person] grabbed the wrong [thing], and [impossible result]." | "An engineer grabbed the wrong part from a box, and his machine started beating like a heart." |
| 3 | **Impossible fact, no explanation:** "[Thing] could [impossible], and [experts] couldn't find the trick." | "A horse in Berlin could do math, and thirteen experts couldn't find the trick." |
| 4 | **Backfire:** "[Group] paid people to [goal], and ended up with [the opposite]." | "A city paid people to kill rats, and ended up with more rats than ever." |
| 5 | **Contradiction:** "[Object] was [state A]... but [state B]." | "A scientist opened a gas tank that was completely empty... and it still weighed full." |
| 6 | **Rejected genius:** "[Thing] was thrown away for being too [quality]. That was the whole point." | "The world's strongest glue was thrown out for being too sticky." |
| 7 | **Absurd first life:** "The [everyday thing] in your [place] started out as [absurd thing]." | "The toy clay in every kids' room started out as wallpaper cleaner." |
| 8 | **You-hook (it's in your life):** "The [thing] you [used today] exists because [absurd cause]." | "The microwave in your kitchen exists because a man ruined his snack." |
| 9 | **Myth-buster (contrarian):** "Everyone thinks [belief]. It actually [truth]." | "Everyone thinks NASA invented Velcro. It actually came from a dog walk in the Alps." |
| 10 | **Shock number:** "The first [thing] cost [$] and weighed as much as [object]. Now [contrast]." | "The first microwave cost five thousand dollars and weighed as much as a piano. Now it's in every kitchen." |

Use 1-5 most: they give an instant picture and a question. Pair any template with a **Magician** frame 1 (the strange thing in
motion) [F].

## 6. The ready-to-paste ChatGPT prompt

> You are writing a YouTube Short script for an animated story channel ([CHANNEL NAME]: [one-line description, e.g.
> "surprising stories behind everyday gadgets"]). The story is original and made-up unless I say it's a true story. The narrator tells it in third person, fast and confident. Topic: [TOPIC].
>
> Rules:
> 1. Make it as long as the story needs and no longer: [LENGTH, e.g. "about 30 seconds (about 110 words)" or "whatever the story
>    needs, 20-60 seconds"]. Roughly 3.7 words per second. One sentence per line, short punchy sentences, everyday words a 10-year-old understands, active voice, no hedging
>    words (maybe, might, could), no dialogue tags, no emojis, no intro, no outro, no "subscribe".
> 2. A made-up story only has to make sense inside its own world. Use specific invented details (names, places, times, numbers);
>    specific beats vague. Never invent crimes or scandals for real, named people, and never present the story as documented history.
>    (If I say it's a true story, keep every fact accurate.)
> 3. Line 1 is the HOOK: the craziest sentence in the whole story, 10-16 words, stated flat as fact, with the strange part in
>    the first 5-8 words, a specific name, year or number, and a built-in contrast between what anyone would expect and what happened.
>    Never start with the title, a year or a time phrase ("In 1964", "Back in", "One day"), background, or "you won't believe"; open on the strange thing itself and put years on screen instead.
> 4. Lines 2-3 confirm the hook with who, where and when, and one concrete detail.
> 5. Then: people doubt or laugh, something is at risk, and 2-4 clues the viewer can guess from. Link every line with "but" or
>    "therefore" logic, never "and then".
> 6. Add a re-hook line about every 15-20 seconds (e.g. "But here's the strange part.", "That should have been the end. It wasn't.").
>    Around the middle, a line starting "Meanwhile," with a parallel clue or second mystery.
> 7. The reveal starts with "You see," and explains the hidden mechanism simply, in 2-4 lines, with one everyday comparison. It must
>    surprise, but every clue from before must make sense now. The reveal should end at about 80-85% of the script.
> 8. After the reveal: one punchy payoff line that lands the point as a complete sentence, then a final line that runs straight into
>    line 1 so the video loops (e.g. "And it all started when..."). No summary, no moral, at most 2-3 lines after the reveal.
> 9. Under the script, give: (a) a 2-4 word on-screen hook text for second 1; (b) the key visual for frame 1; (c) a short on-screen
>    comment question for the last 3 seconds; (d) a pinned comment question; (e) 3 title options under 50 characters that keep the
>    twist secret; (f) the two clues you planted for the twist and where they appear.

## 7. Before voicing, and before rendering

**Script check (before voicing):** run section 1 on line 1; check the shape in section 2 (reveal ends at 80-85%, ≤8 s after it,
two clues planted, a re-hook every 15-20 s, "but/therefore" everywhere); check the loop line; check the two planted clues (and, only for a true story, its facts).

**First-second check (before rendering):** render frame 0, frame 15 and frame 45 of the Short as stills and run section 1's picture,
text and sound boxes on them. If frame 1 doesn't already show the strange thing in motion, rebuild the first shot.

**After posting:** read the retention graph. A cliff in the first 3 seconds means the hook failed; a slide in the middle means
missing re-hooks; a drop after the reveal means the tail is too long. Write what you learn into `docs/lessons/`.
