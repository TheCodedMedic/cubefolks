The story, one beat per line: a horse in Berlin in 1904 who could do maths (true story, Clever Hans). His owner, a retired maths teacher (Wilhelm von Osten: white beard, black hat), shows him off in a courtyard. Hans answers by tapping his hoof ("Two plus three? Five taps." / "The square root of sixteen? Four taps, and a proud little snort."). Scientists come to expose the trick and find nothing; strangers ask the questions and he's still right. Then "Meanwhile" a young student, Oskar, finds that Hans fails when the asker doesn't know the answer, or when Hans wears blinders. "You see," Hans couldn't count: the people watching tensed up as the hoof neared the right number, then relaxed and lifted their heads a tiny bit on the final tap, and Hans stopped. Even Oskar couldn't stop doing it. It's called the Clever Hans effect, and it's why experiments are run blind. The last line ("...the whole world believed") loops straight back into line 1, so the final frame must cut seamlessly into the first.
