In 1904, a horse in Berlin could do math better than most schoolchildren.

His name was Hans, and he answered by tapping his hoof.

Two plus three? Five taps.

The square root of sixteen? Four taps, and a proud little snort.

His owner, a retired math teacher, swore he had taught him everything himself.

Scientists rolled their eyes and came to expose the trick.

They searched for hidden signals, secret helpers, anything at all.

Nothing.

They sent the owner away and let strangers ask the questions.

Hans still got them right.

Meanwhile, a young student named Oskar noticed something strange.

When the person asking didn't know the answer, Hans got it wrong.

And when Hans wore blinders, his genius vanished completely.

You see, Hans couldn't count at all.

He was counting on us.

As his hoof got close to the right number, everyone watching tensed up without realizing it.

On the final tap, they relaxed, their heads lifted just a tiny bit, and Hans stopped.

Even Oskar, who knew the secret, couldn't stop himself from doing it.

Scientists still call it the Clever Hans effect, and it's why experiments today are run blind.

So Hans was a genius after all, just not at math.

He was a genius at reading people.

And we were so easy to read that the whole world believed...
