In 1902, a city paid people to kill rats, and ended up with more rats than ever.

The city was Hanoi, and its shiny new sewers had become a highway for rats.

Rats popped out of toilets in the fanciest homes in town.

So the officials made a simple offer.

Bring us a rat tail, and we'll pay you one cent.

Tails poured in by the thousands.

Some days, more than twenty thousand of them.

The officials cheered, counting tails in big wooden crates.

But the streets were still crawling.

And people started noticing something odd.

Rats were running around with no tails.

Meanwhile, outside the city, inspectors found barns full of rats, fed and cared for like pets.

You see, the city wasn't paying for dead rats.

It was paying for tails.

And a rat doesn't need its tail to make more rats.

So clever hunters caught a rat, snipped off its tail, and let it go.

Every rat they freed had babies, and every baby grew a brand new tail.

Some people stopped hunting altogether and simply farmed them.

The more the city paid, the more rats it got.

Economists call this a perverse incentive.

When you reward the proof instead of the result, people just make more proof.

Hanoi quietly cancelled the bounty.
